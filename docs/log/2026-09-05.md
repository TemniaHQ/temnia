**Day 0 — post 2**

Okay, so what is Temnia?

Imagine you run a content agency and a client sends you a two-hour podcast.

By the end of the week, that one recording has to become a clean episode, chapters, segment videos, shorts, quotes, show notes and posts. Someone has to keep track of what the client approved, which version is current, and whether the clip actually says what the caption claims.

The tools in this space are mostly chasing the same thing: find a few exciting thirty-second moments.

I kept coming back to the part they leave behind. The whole episode still has to be understood and cut by hand.

That's where Temnia starts.

It cuts the recording into complete chapters first. Every second belongs to one chapter or is deliberately dropped. Two neighbouring chapters share one boundary, so a bad cut damages both of them. Once that chapter spine is right, clips, quotes and everything else can hang from it, with a path back to the exact seconds they came from.

Nothing publishes because a model felt confident. A person approves it.

That's the product: less a clip generator, more a cutting room for the whole episode.

**Day 1 — post 3**

I should get one uncomfortable fact out of the way: this is my second attempt at Temnia.

The first codebase reached 120 commits. Then, on 28 August, I ran the test that mattered.

It picked 40 clips from a fresh 90-minute episode. Editors accepted 7.

Seven.

The model often found an interesting idea, then started the clip before the thought had begun or ended it after the energy was gone. I had rebuilt the exact complaint I was trying to solve.

I could have spent another month patching around it. Instead I archived the repo.

That wasn't fun, but 18% wasn't a rough edge. The system had the wrong shape.

So this rebuild starts with the evaluation harness and the boundary problem, not the dashboard. Eighteen percent is the number on the wall. When I run that test again, I'll post the result even if it is still embarrassing.

**Day 2 — post 4**

The failed version left me with one rule for this rebuild: a model never chooses a timestamp.

A model can say, “This is the part worth keeping.” But ask it to start at exactly 13:42.6 and it will confidently clip the first syllable or leave a dead breath at the end. An editor notices immediately.

So Temnia's model chooses sentence IDs. That's all. Ordinary code maps them to word timings, looks at pauses and neighbouring segments, and decides where the cut can safely land. Then another evaluator checks the result, and a person still has the final say.

It's a lot less glamorous than asking a larger model to do everything. I'm okay with that.

Will it beat 18%? I don't know yet. That's what I'm building now.
